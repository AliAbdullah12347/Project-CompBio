1. Overview — everything we did, in one page

The problem. Nearly every bipolar patient in a public dataset is medicated, so any “bipolar signature” found in blood may simply be a lithium signature. Lithium raises neutrophil counts, and that alone shifts bulk gene expression without any cell changing its behaviour. We set out to separate the two.

What we had. 480 whole-blood RNA-seq samples from GSE124326 (Krebs et al., 2020), reduced to a 474-subject analysis cohort: 226 bipolar I, 14 bipolar II, 234 controls.

What we ran. Two contrasts × two levels of resolution:

  • Lithium: 152 users vs 74 non-users, within bipolar I only (n = 226). Diagnosis is held constant, so anything found is attributable to the drug.
  • Bipolar: 74 bipolar I patients off lithium vs 234 controls (n = 308). Medication is held constant, so anything found is attributable to the illness.
  • Each measured in whole blood (what the sequencer actually read) and in five cell lineages (granulocytes, monocytes, T, NK, B — estimated computationally).

What we found.

  • Lithium has a large, reproducible effect on whole blood: 1,426 genes. 125 survive the strictest possible correction; 894 are called under every one of 31 different gene-filtering choices.
  • Bipolar disorder, off lithium, has essentially none: 4 genes, all sitting on the significance boundary (q = 0.049 against a 0.05 cutoff), none surviving a stricter correction.
  • Most of the lithium signal is cell composition, not changed cell behaviour: 92.8% of the 1,426 genes move in exactly the direction that the granulocyte fraction alone predicts.
  • But the core of it is not. All 125 of the highest-confidence genes survive statistical adjustment for cell composition. Composition explains the breadth of the signature, not its centre.
  • At cell-type resolution the lithium effect sits in granulocytes: 109 of 161 significant gene×lineage results.
  • Nothing for bipolar at cell-type resolution — and we show in section 11 that this zero carries no information, because the method cannot detect effects of the size we care about.

What we checked. Thirty-one gene filters, ten multiple-testing corrections, permutation nulls throughout, a spike-in power analysis, the three follow-ups raised at the 18 October meeting, and two further gene-filtering strategies tested across nine cutoffs and twelve analyses. Section 10 holds the methodological findings, two of which matter beyond this project: the cell-type estimates are so strongly dependent across genes that the number of discoveries is an unstable statistic (10.2), and variance-based gene filtering — a reasonable-sounding idea — actively destroys signal here, for a reason we can state exactly (10.5).

One new result came out of that filtering work, and it is the cleanest statement of the bipolar finding we have: unmedicated bipolar I differs modestly from controls in leukocyte composition — including a reduced basophil/mast compartment (2.36% against 2.13%, p = 0.039) — and we find no whole-blood transcriptional signature independent of it. Two different filters, applied independently, both land on composition (sections 10.5 and 10.6).

2. What “significant” means here

Each gene is tested separately, so one comparison runs 12,368 tests. If absolutely nothing were happening, testing each at the conventional p < 0.05 would still flag about 618 genes by chance alone. A list built that way would be mostly noise.

So we control the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure at 0.05. The promise it makes is precise, and narrower than people usually assume:

  • Of the genes on our list, we expect roughly 5% to be there by chance. On a list of 1,426 that is about 71 expected false positives.
  • It does not promise that the list is 95% likely to be entirely correct. That is a much stricter guarantee (family-wise error rate), which Holm's procedure provides, and which we report separately.
  • It says nothing about whether an effect is large or biologically important. A gene can be highly significant and barely move.

So: a significant gene is one whose between-group difference is bigger than noise can comfortably explain, after accounting for having looked at 12,368 genes at once.

3. The data and the four comparisons

3.1 How 480 samples became 474

The depositors flagged 444 of 480 samples as passing their QC. We did not use that flag, because it is not random with respect to diagnosis: it drops 35 of 240 controls, 1 of 14 bipolar II, and 0 of 226 bipolar I (Fisher exact p = 3.0×10−10). Using it would mean every case–control comparison ran against a filtered control group, for reasons unrelated to the biology.

Taking the exclusions apart, 32 of the 36 were dropped only for missing demographic data (mostly tobacco status) and 4 were genuine sample mix-ups. So:

  • 4 sample mix-ups removed — these cannot be recovered.
  • 2 removed for missing data too extensive to impute.
  • 30 recovered by multiple imputation of tobacco status, which is missing-at-random and not a reason to discard an otherwise complete transcriptome.

Result: 474 subjects — 226 bipolar I, 14 bipolar II, 234 controls.

3.3 Covariates, and proof that they matter

Every model adjusts for age, sex, tobacco use, RNA integrity (RIN), sequencing plate, and three sequencing-quality components. The bipolar model additionally adjusts for assessment group; the lithium model cannot, because within bipolar I that variable is constant.

These are not decorative. Removing them changes the answer:

  • Bipolar, whole blood, without covariates: 54 genes.
  • Bipolar, whole blood, with covariates: 4 genes.
  • The two gene rankings correlate at only r = 0.654.

Fifty of those 54 genes were tracking age, smoking or batch rather than diagnosis. Within the lithium contrast the covariates are better behaved — tobacco is balanced (35.1% vs 31.6%, p = 0.70) and sequencing plate is balanced (p = 0.97) — but age is not (median 54.8 in non-users vs 48.9 in users, p = 0.0034), which is reason enough to keep adjusting.

4.1 Why this filter

The cutoff (>10 counts in ≥90% of samples, giving 12,368 genes) was chosen in advance and then tested against 30 alternatives spanning everything from no filter at all to a very aggressive one, including edgeR::filterByExpr (13,925 genes). Across all 31, 894 of the 1,426 lithium genes are called every single time. The conclusion does not depend on where the line is drawn, which is the only defensible reason to pick one.

For reference, Krebs et al. report 12,344 genes from a nominally identical rule. Our 12,368 differs by 24, which is an annotation-version difference, not a pipeline error.

4.2 How the cell-type estimates were made

We do not have measured cell counts. They are estimated in two stages:

  • CIBERSORTx with the LM22 signature (B-mode batch correction, quantile normalisation off, 100 permutations) estimates what fraction of each sample is each of 22 cell types. We collapsed those 22 into 5 lineages — granulocytes, monocytes, T, NK, B — because at 22-type resolution the estimates are not reliable (ICC 0.389); at 5 lineages they are acceptable (ICC 0.693).
  • bMIND then estimates what each lineage is expressing in each individual sample, giving a 12,368 × 5 × 474 array. We ran it without supplying the phenotype, so it cannot have been told the answer it was being asked for.

Section 10 shows that these cell-type estimates have a serious statistical property that limits what can be concluded from them. The whole-blood results do not depend on them at all.

7. Ranking the genes (no significance threshold)

Why do this at all. A significance cutoff throws away information. A gene ranked 12th in granulocytes, 30th in monocytes and 25th in T cells is clearly doing something, yet it may fail BH in all three because each test is individually underpowered. Ranking sidesteps the threshold entirely: instead of asking is this gene significant? we ask how high does it come, consistently, across the lineages?

The method — rank product. For each gene, take its rank by p-value within each of the five lineages and multiply them (equivalently, average the logs). A gene that is 10th in all five scores far better than one that is 1st in one lineage and 9,000th in the others. This rewards consistency rather than a single lucky lineage.

What the null says

Most of the agreement is shared structure, as predicted — but not quite all of it. Scrambled labels produce a median cross-lineage correlation of 0.618 to 0.679, against an observed 0.730 to 0.784. So the great majority of the “0.556 to 0.871 agreement” headline is a property of the estimates rather than of the biology: five lineages built from one bulk matrix, sharing an axis that carries 71% of their variance, agree strongly whether or not the group labels mean anything.

The residual gap is not nothing, and it is entirely in the lithium contrast. 5 of the 16 statistics nominally exceed their null, and all 5 of them are lithium; the bipolar contrast returns 0 of 8 on either ranking. The strongest is the multi-lineage overlap: 85 genes reach the top 100 of three or more lineages against a null median of 35 (recentred ranking, p = 0.0099), and 68 against 40 on the raw ranking (p = 0.0198).

But none of it survives the multiplicity it created. Those 16 p-values come from 4 statistics × 2 rankings × 2 contrasts, all decided after seeing the data. The smallest is 0.0099 against a Benjamini–Hochberg threshold of 0.0031, and nothing clears it — nor Bonferroni at 0.0031. Having spent this whole document insisting that counts be priced for the searches that produced them, we apply the same standard here.

The sentence to write

A rank product across the five lineages gives a usable ordering of genes, but the cross-lineage agreement it rests on is largely a property of the deconvolution rather than evidence of independent replication. For the lithium contrast there is a suggestion of genuine excess agreement, concentrated in how many genes rank highly in three or more lineages at once; it does not survive correction for the comparisons made and should be treated as hypothesis-generating. For the bipolar contrast there is no excess at all.

What changes in practice. The rankings in 7.1 and 7.2 stay — an ordering is still the right way to choose which genes to look at first, and it is the only cell-type output the bipolar contrast has. What must go is any sentence offering “the lineages agree” as support for a gene. On the evidence here that agreement is mostly the same measurement repeated five times.

One encouraging detail, for honesty in the other direction: the asymmetry between the contrasts is itself a check on the method. If the test were simply registering shared structure it would do so equally in both, since both use the same estimates and overlapping samples. It fires only where a real drug effect is known to exist and stays silent where we have independently concluded there is nothing to find. That is the behaviour a working test should show.

9. Is it just cell composition?

This is the question the whole project exists to answer, so it deserves a direct answer: largely yes, but not entirely, and the part that is not is the strongest part.

9.1 The evidence that it IS composition

  • 92.8% of the 1,426 lithium genes move in exactly the direction predicted by that gene's correlation with granulocyte fraction alone.
  • The median gene loses 40.8% of its effect once cell composition is held fixed.
  • The effect is strongly one-directional: 66% of the 1,426 genes go up (945 against 481), and 120 of the 125 highest-confidence genes go up. A shift in the cell mixture pushes most genes the same way; a specific transcriptional response would not.
  • At cell-type resolution the effect concentrates in granulocytes (109 of 161), the very compartment lithium is known to expand.

9.2 The evidence that it is NOT only composition

  • All 125 Holm-significant genes survive adjustment for composition. Not most — all of them.
  • Survival falls steadily as the genes get weaker: 100% in the top decile, then 62.9%, 17.6%, and on down to 1.4% in the bottom decile. Composition accounts for the long tail of the signature, not its core.
  • This is not circular reasoning. If the adjustment were simply erasing small effects, the effect size and the percent of effect lost would correlate. They correlate at 0.026.

9.3 The conclusion to write down

Lithium use is associated with a large and highly reproducible whole-blood expression signature. The majority of that signature is attributable to a shift in leukocyte composition — principally an expansion of the granulocyte compartment — but a core of roughly 125 genes remains significant after composition is held fixed, indicating a component of the effect that the mixture alone does not explain.

We tested the obvious alternative and it failed: if composition were masking a real signal, adjusting for it should flip the sign of a meaningful number of genes. We set a bar of 10% sign discordance in advance. The observed figure was 0.000%.

This is the single most important technical finding in the cell-type arm. In the bMIND granulocyte estimates one sample-level axis explains 71% of all variance, and any two genes correlate at 0.67 on average. The 12,368 genes are close to being one variable measured 12,368 times.

The consequences are concrete:

  • When a permutation's labels happen to line up with that one axis, every gene shifts together and BH converts a single sample-level coincidence into a thousand gene-level discoveries.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg assumes independence or positive regression dependence. Under correlation this strong its guarantee is not reliable, and the number of discoveries becomes a highly unstable statistic.
  • The strong cross-lineage agreement reported in section 7 is substantially this shared axis rather than independent biological replication.
  • It is a property of the data, not a defect in the code, and it applies to the raw and centred analyses alike.

  • The 161 raw cell-type lithium findings stand as the headline cell-type result, with the dependence stated as a limitation. In the lineages where it matters the observed count beats its own permutation null (granulocytes p = 0.030, T cells p = 0.020).
  • The 284 / 345 centred counts must not be reported as findings. The bipolar granulocyte null alone reaches 34.5 genes under the same correction.
  • The exact count is not a stable quantity in these estimates and should not be emphasised. The pattern — granulocyte-dominated, lithium-specific — is far more robust than the number attached to it.
  • The whole-blood results are untouched by all of this. They are computed from the measured expression matrix and never pass through bMIND.

10.4 Filtering genes by standard deviation

We checked that independence requirement first, and it passes: the correlation between a gene's standard deviation and its test statistic under the null is below 0.04 in every lineage. The filter is legitimate.

10.5 The same filter pushed much harder — cut to 200 genes

The proposal. Take the variance filter in 10.4 far further: keep only the ~200 most variable genes and test within those. The reasoning is that a gene which barely varies across the whole cohort cannot differ much between two subgroups of it, so those genes can be discarded with confidence; and with only 200 tests instead of 12,368 the Benjamini–Hochberg threshold for the top gene relaxes by a factor of 62, which should let real but modest effects surface.

The logic is correct and the precondition holds. We measured that rather than assuming it: the Spearman correlation between a gene's SD and its significance under permuted labels is +0.024 (lithium) and +0.007 (bipolar) — essentially zero. Verdict on the 200-gene variance filter

  • Do not use it for the lithium analyses. It is statistically valid and it destroys the result: 1,426 → 10 in whole blood, 161 → 7 at cell-type level, with the three strongest genes in the study discarded.
  • For whole-blood bipolar the answer is split. At the 200-gene cut it is 4 → 0. At a 50-gene cut it returns 5 genes that survive a selection-aware null (p = 0.015) and point at a real reduction in the basophil/mast compartment — the one genuine yield of the whole filtering exercise, and, not coincidentally, the one analysis that had almost no signal for the filter to destroy.
  • The bipolar T-cell finding is worth one paragraph, heavily hedged. Eleven genes, permutation p = 0.018, of which 4 survive full composition adjustment: RP11-561P12.5, TUBB1, FCER1A, RP11-704M14.1. Report it as hypothesis-generating, with the marker enrichment stated. It is a lead, not a result.
  • The general lesson is worth stating in the paper. Variance-based filtering is a reasonable-sounding idea that this dataset lets us test directly, and it fails for a specific, explainable reason. That is a useful methodological observation in its own right.

10.6 The filter that should have worked — mean expression

Why try a different statistic. Section 10.5 showed that filtering on standard deviation fails for a specific reason: total variance is dominated by within-group noise, so ranking on it ranks on the denominator of the t-statistic. The underlying idea — spend an independent filter to buy a less punishing correction — survives that finding intact. It just needs a statistic tied to power rather than to noise.

Mean expression is the textbook choice. Bourgon, Gentleman and Huber (2010) show that for a two-group comparison the overall mean is independent of the t-statistic under the null — the precondition SD satisfies only approximately — while still being strongly tied to power, because low-count genes carry most of the measurement noise. Dropping them removes tests that had little chance of succeeding without removing candidates that did. It is what DESeq2 does by default.

A prediction we wrote down first. We expected the gain here to be small, for two reasons specific to this pipeline: the baseline filter (>10 counts in ≥90% of samples) has already removed the low-expression genes, so the pool mean filtering normally targets is largely gone; and voom already down-weights low-count observations through the mean-variance trend, so part of the benefit is being collected twice.

T cells: composition, unambiguously. Every one of the 154 goes down, they are 7.3× enriched for LM22 signature genes, and not one survives adjustment for cell fractions.   • The gene classes are wrong for biology. 68 of the 86 (79%) are snoRNAs, scaRNAs, ribosomal-protein genes, histones or pseudogenes — SCARNA10, SNORA53, RPL39P3, RPS23P8, RPL32, RPS21, HIST1H4C — against 27% among the 1,000 genes tested, a 3.0× enrichment. That is the profile of a library-composition effect, not a B-cell response.
  • RNA integrity does not explain it, which we checked rather than assumed: the hits correlate with RIN less than the average tested gene (median |r| smaller, Wilcoxon p = 6×10−7), and all 86 remain significant when RIN is fitted as a 4-degree-of-freedom spline instead of a single linear term.
  • The decisive objection is internal. Section 11.2 now measures the B-cell detection floor on this very contrast at 16.96 log2FC — about a 130,000-fold change. A lineage in which the method cannot reliably detect a five-fold change (power 0.015 at log2FC 5.0) cannot credibly return 86 genes with effects of 0.1 or so. The result contradicts our own power analysis, and the power analysis is the more trustworthy of the two.

Conclusion: the B-cell estimates from bMIND are not usable, and the 86 genes are a property of those estimates rather than of B cells. This is the strongest single piece of evidence that the cell-type arm should be reported with the dependence and reliability caveats of section 10.2 attached to every claim.

11.2 Cell type — the bipolar zero is uninformative

Here it reverses. We measured the detection floor by spike-in: inject an effect of known size where it actually lives — in the bulk, scaled by that sample's lineage fraction — then re-run the deconvolution and the test, and find the smallest effect reliably recovered. The fourth column explains why, and is the more useful result. Converting each floor back to the whole-blood effect it corresponds to (floor × mean lineage fraction) gives 0.170 to 0.254 across all five lineages — tightly clustered around the whole-blood floor of 0.208. In other words the cell-type analysis is not detecting anything the bulk analysis could not; it is the same sensitivity, divided by each lineage's share of the mixture. A lineage that is 1.5% of the sample needs an effect roughly 67 times larger to clear the same bar. That is a property of deconvolution itself, not of bMIND or of this dataset, and it is worth stating in the paper.

The prediction bears that out: floors predicted purely as 0.208 / fraction were 0.56, 0.76, 0.82, 2.48 and 13.86, against measured values of 0.56, 0.69, 0.77, 2.03 and 16.96. Four of five land within 20%.

This also reframes the 161 lithium findings: they were detectable because lithium's cell-type effects are unusually large, not because the method is sensitive.

12. Limitations and what is still open

12.1 Limitations of the data

  • No lithium dose, serum level or duration. The contrast is use versus non-use, a blunt instrument.
  • No record of other medications. Patients not on lithium are generally taking something else, so this is lithium versus other treatment, not lithium versus nothing.
  • No measured cell counts. Everything about composition is estimated from the same expression matrix being analysed. A complete blood count would answer the central question directly; its absence is the single biggest weakness of the design.
  • Cross-sectional. No causal direction can be established.
  • LM22 misfits whole blood. Against reference intervals the deconvolution puts monocytes at 24.6% (reference 2–8%) and eosinophils at 0.0%. Collapsing to five lineages mitigates this but does not fix it.
  • 74 unmedicated patients is a small group to carry the bipolar contrast.
  • Tobacco status was imputed for 30 subjects.

12.2 Limitations of the analysis

  • The cell-type estimates are strongly dependent across genes (section 10.2). This is the most serious one and it constrains every cell-type claim in this document. The whole-blood results are unaffected.
  • The four bipolar whole-blood genes sit on the threshold (q = 0.0489 against a 0.05 cutoff) and should not be presented as findings.
  • Cell-type effect sizes are not trustworthy in absolute terms given the offset documented in section 10.1, even though the rankings are informative.

12.3 Still open — the honest list

  • The rank-product permutation null has not been run. Closed, and it changed the reading. The cross-lineage agreement the ranking rests on is no greater than chance once the shared structure of the cell-type estimates is accounted for (section 7.3). The rankings remain usable as an ordering, but not as evidence.
  • The bipolar cell-type power analysis is incomplete. Closed. All five lineages are now measured directly on the bipolar contrast (section 11.2); the floors run from 0.56 to 16.96 log2FC and none is interpretable against the 0.5 bar set in advance.
  • The standard-deviation filter sweep (section 10.4) was evaluated on centred data, so its numbers inherit the caveat in section 10.2. The 200-gene version (section 10.5) was run on uncentred data and is unaffected. Neither is recommended for the main analyses.
  • The bipolar cell-type signals produced by filtering are resolved, negatively. At the larger 2,000-gene cut the T-cell set is 154 genes, all in one direction, 7.3× enriched for LM22 markers, and none survives composition adjustment (section 10.6). Nothing there needs replicating.
  • The B-cell lineage estimates are not usable. They return 86 genes that are neither composition nor RNA integrity, in a lineage whose own detection floor is 26.11 log2FC (sections 10.6 and 11.2). Both cannot be true. This belongs in the paper as a limitation of bMIND on this dataset rather than as an open question.
  • Whether any cell-type analysis can be made fully calibrated on these estimates is unresolved. Three corrections were tried; none fixed it.
